# Quantum Pathways: A World Beneath Physics

**Unified Temporal Field, Multiverse Layer, and Spacetime-State Synchronization**

**Concept Author:** Semperfive (Alexander Negots)  
**Version:** 7.1 (for publication) · **Date:** 04.10.2026  
**Status:** Conceptual framework for a science fiction universe

> **Important.** This is a model for fiction and a thought experiment, not a scientific theory. It uses terms and ideas from modern physics (entanglement, vacuum fluctuations, causality), but is unproven and makes no claim to describe the real Universe.

> **Terms of use** (Section 41) — a draft, open for discussion. Writing stories in this world is free; figures and mechanisms for royalties are still under discussion.

## The World in One Page

Humanity has learned to travel between stars almost instantaneously. A ship does not accelerate or convert into data: it changes its spatial binding along a path that already exists in the multiverse layer.

This path has one condition — an **anchor**: a pair of entangled particles. One half must be physically transported to the other end by conventional flight, taking centuries. An anchor cannot be carried through a channel: transition destroys entanglement. Therefore, civilization expands in slow waves, while within already connected territory everything happens instantly. Communication between systems is a "telegraph": a stream of hydrogen and helium atoms through each anchor, line by line.

There are no contrived conveniences in this world. A lost anchor means years of isolation. A failed second pulse means losing yourself. The trace of every transition is visible. From these limitations, stories grow:

- A ship reached its destination but could not fix itself in place.
- An expedition deployed anchors, everything works, but there is no one left on Earth.
- A colony lost its only anchor and waits three hundred years for help.
- A hub decides which messages will reach the neighboring colony.

The concept author deliberately does not write stories: he designs a place where other people's destinies unfold. Here, you can write your own story.

## About This Document

The document describes a universe built on a single physical assumption and its consequences. It is a foundation for authors: it allows building plots without breaking the fundamentals.

**Structure** moves from physics to consequences and overlays:

- **Part I. Basic Principles** — the physics of the world.
- **Part II. Consequences** — logical deductions from the principles. The sole exception is Section 29: it describes a future discovery that the principles permit but do not derive.
- **Part III. Overlays** — economics and other structures built on top of consequences. Not mandatory: a specific civilization may arrange things differently.
- **Part IV. State of the Concept** — open questions, position, summary.
- **Part V. Creative Principles and Sandbox** — story prompts, canon rules, and terms of use for authors.

**What is immutable.** Parts I and II are not revised in substance. Only clarifications and contradiction fixes are permitted; these are issued as a new version, recorded in version history, and do not override already established canon.

**How to read.** A reader needs "The World in One Page" and the story prompts (Section 38). An author needs Parts I, II, and V. A canon arbiter needs the entire document.

**Terms.**

- **Anchor** — a pair of entangled particles that defines the transition path.
- **Stabilizer** — a device that protects the anchor particle from decoherence.
- **Modulators** — devices that act on the states of the object during transition.
- **AI Synchronizer** (synchronizer) — a system that aligns the object's states with the path.
- **First and second pulses** — channel capture and fixation at the destination.
- **Microchannel** — communication via a stream of atoms through an anchor.
- **Hub** — a network node through which intercolonial traffic flows.

## Part I. Basic Principles

### 1. The Multiverse Layer

The world consists of many physically existing universes that overlay each other and are connected by a shared medium — the **multiverse layer**. This is not a mathematical abstraction or a "parallel corridor" next to our world, but a physical medium outside conventional three-dimensional geometry. Distances in it have a different metric: a path between two points millions of light-years apart in ordinary space may be short. Interaction between universes is weak.

All universes originated from a single primordial event (conventionally, the Big Bang). Therefore, they share similar or identical laws, develop synchronously, but not identically. Small differences between them are the source of vacuum fluctuations (Section 3), weak interlayer interaction, and transit traces (Section 12).

### 2. The Unified Temporal Field

A unified temporal field operates in all universes. Time flows only from past to future, closed time loops are impossible, and gravity and velocity only change the local rate of time. Transition through the layer is not time travel.

This is a postulate of the model: it sets a single direction of time, so superluminal transition does not lead into the past (Section 14).

### 3. Spin, Path, and Entanglement

Every particle is connected to the layer, and spin is the carrier of that connection: it traverses a physical path linking the corresponding states of particles.

**The infinite tree problem.** From common origin, it follows that an ordinary particle is connected to all universes at once, and each universe to all others. Attempting to trace the path of such a particle yields a branching tree extending to infinity: the path is undefined, the calculation diverges.

**Entanglement narrows the problem.** An entangled particle has a single defined path to one specific particle in one specific universe. Entanglement "cuts out" one route from an infinite multitude, and the calculation becomes finite. In our physics, this is observed as quantum entanglement. In this model, it is not signal transmission through space but a manifestation of an already existing path, and it carries no information by itself.

**Consequence.** Without an entangled particle, transition is impossible not physically but informationally: the system cannot select one path from an infinite multitude. Paths always exist, but choosing the right one requires an anchor.

**Vacuum fluctuations** are weak, brief manifestations of the same path trees. They are always present but form no stable channel: without entanglement, there is no defined route. Entanglement and fluctuations are two modes of the same phenomenon.

### 4. The Anchor

An **anchor** is an entangled particle (or system of particles), one half of which is at the point of departure, the other at the point of destination. It serves three interrelated functions:

1. **Spatial coordinate** — where the object should be fixed.
2. **State reference** — which universe and which state of that universe this point binds to. A single geometric coordinate is insufficient: the path passes through a practically infinite number of universes.
3. **Computational filter** — reduces the infinite tree of paths to a single route (Section 3).

The third function grounds the first two: the coordinate and reference are defined only because the path is defined. The reference is set at the moment the pair is created.

An anchor is bidirectional: transition is possible in both directions (symmetry of entanglement). It is not consumed by transitions, so one anchor serves repeatedly. Each anchor pair is a separate channel, and channels are independent of each other. Hence the distinction: **the anchor is infrastructure, the synchronizer is the operational system** (Section 9). The main limitation is not the anchor itself but the synchronizer's ability to use it correctly.

### 5. Creating and Maintaining an Anchor

Entanglement is created by particle contact and **cannot be established at a distance**. A pair is entangled in one place, then one half is physically transported to the target system by conventional means — years or centuries. Therefore, the first access to a remote system is always conventional, and the fast route appears only after the anchor is installed. The sole possible exception is the discovery described in Section 29.

Each half of an anchor requires a **stabilizer**: a device that isolates the particle from external influences and maintains correlation with its partner. The main risk is decoherence: if entanglement is destroyed, the anchor stops working. Partial degradation can be corrected by resynchronizing the stabilizers; full decoherence is irreversible without bringing the particles together again — that is, without a new anchor.

An anchor is assembled from a stabilizer, an isolation system, a power system, and a synchronization system. In colonies without developed industry, it can be installed but not built.

### 6. An Anchor Cannot Be Carried Through a Channel

Two anchors can be placed in the same system, but **a new anchor cannot be sent through an existing channel**. The first pulse fixes the states of the transported object's particles relative to the particle on the receiving side. When the state of an entangled particle is fixed to a new anchor, its bond with its partner is destroyed: the particle arrives intact but ordinary.

> **An anchor cannot be carried through a channel: transition destroys the entanglement that makes an anchor an anchor.**

Consequences:

- **Delivery of a new anchor is always by conventional means**: an expedition of years or centuries. This is not a separate rule but a consequence of transition mechanics.
- **No exponential expansion.** Otherwise, civilization would cover the galaxy in a few years: set up, jump, set up further. Expansion remains wavelike (Section 18). The exception is only within already connected territory and only after the discovery in Section 29.
- **No "secret anchor networks."** A smuggled anchor was physically delivered by someone (Section 20).
- **Short jumps between already-placed markers work.** If a system has multiple anchors, once delivered by conventional means, moving between them is a small-scale task.

### 7. Anchor in Open Space

An object exits transition at the anchor point. If another object is nearby (station, planet, ship), the consequences are catastrophic. The atoms of both objects end up in the same place, bonds break, and the structure of both is destroyed. Even without this, the velocity difference produces a collision at cosmic speed (Section 14).

> **Anchors are always placed in open space, at a calculated distance from any objects. Not by rule, but because otherwise the transition kills both the arrival and what they arrived at.**

Consequences:

- **Two-tier transport.** The layer handles large distances; conventional navigation handles the final thousands of kilometers. Both are needed.
- **Vulnerability window.** Between "we have transitioned" and "we are docked," the ship travels on conventional engines without transition protection. This is a ready-made scene for interception.
- **Passive station defense.** If the adversary has no anchor in your system, they cannot jump to the station.
- **A secret anchor near a station is a weapon.** But delivering such an anchor takes years or centuries, and the station's position relative to it remains unknown until the moment of transition.

**Stationary and mobile anchors.** A stationary anchor is fixed relative to a planet or star. A mobile anchor is placed on a separate platform ("anchor buoy") that moves with its own vector. Neither is mounted on the hull of a ship or station: the arriving object would end up inside it.

### 8. Macroscopic and Biological Objects

Any object is a vast number of interrelated states (atoms, molecules, electronic states, bonds, macrostructure) that must be aligned with each other. A ship cannot be moved as a point: its entire structure must be synchronized. The more complex the object, the more complex the synchronization.

A biological object is primarily a structure: molecular bonds, chemical gradients, neural networks, membrane potentials. It is defined by the mutual arrangement of atoms, not by the spins of individual particles. Transition fixes spins for passage through the channel but does not break bonds or alter the arrangement of atoms.

- **Nominal transition:** the heart does not skip a beat, neural connections are not severed, processes are not interrupted.
- **Emergency transition:** damage exactly proportional to the loss of synchronization. A few atoms — unnoticeable; failure of a modulator group — tissue damage; full collapse — death.
- **State is irrelevant.** A person in a coma, under anesthesia, or in cryosleep transitions just as well. Only the number and complexity of states matter.

### 9. The Synchronizer: AI and Modulators

A human cannot manage that many states. **Modulators** act on the states of the object's components. The **AI synchronizer** reads and aligns the object's state in real time, computes the control signal, transmits it to the modulators, holds the object in a coherent state, controls the transition, calculates the counteraction, fixes the object relative to the anchor, and breaks the original synchronization. Anchor stabilizers (Section 5) are separate devices.

The AI is not a navigator or an interface but a part of the movement mechanism on equal footing with the anchor. It decides which channel to use, what degree of synchronization is achievable, where heightened attention is needed, how to allocate resources, and when to execute the second pulse (not by clock but by the state of the object relative to the anchor). The pilot sets the goal; the crew trusts the AI not with navigation but with the integrity of their own bodies.

### 10. Three Stages of Transition

**I. Capture (first pulse).** Modulators shift the object from its normal state to a synchronized state and capture the channel. This is not acceleration but a restructuring of state.

**II. Transit.** The spatial binding changes. The object travels along a different metric and does not overcome distance, resistance, or inertial load; from the outside, it appears almost instantaneous. The object remains physically continuous: it is not destroyed and reassembled, and information is not transmitted separately from matter. What moves is the physical state of the object itself.

**III. Fixation (second pulse).** The second pulse removes the original synchronization (conventionally, a "superposition with the opposite phase" of the original state) and fixes the object in the state defined by the anchor. The AI must know the parameters of the first pulse to correctly form the second.

> **Movement ends not when the object has arrived "in the right place," but when it has gained stable belonging to the anchor's universe.**

### 11. Fixation Failure

If the object reaches the target but the second pulse is not executed in time, it remains bound to the layer and loses full fixation.

- **Partial state loss:** some elements fall out of synchronization — structural damage, loss of atoms or bonds.
- **Incomplete synchronization:** the object is fixed in neither the original nor the new configuration; in the limit, it loses unambiguous spatial belonging.
- **Multiverse scattering:** at final collapse, parts of the object receive different final states and may end up bound to different universes. This is not an explosion but a loss of unified spatiotemporal-state belonging.

Failure gradation: modulator — local desynchronization; modulator group — cascading loss of synchronization; AI — object disintegration; second pulse — the most dangerous failure; full collapse — scattering.

### 12. Transit Trace

After the object departs, a perceptible quantum noise remains at the point of origin — **the echo of severed bonds with the layer's paths**. The more particles, the more bonds were established and severed, the stronger the trace.

- **There is no covert transition.** The trace reveals the fact of transition and roughly the scale of the object: a weak trace — a probe; a strong trace — a large ship.
- **Intelligence and forensics.** Traces can be used to estimate what passes through a channel and to investigate incidents, illegal channels, and military operations.
- **The noise floor of active anchors** affects sensitive equipment and reveals channel activity.
- This is the same phenomenon as vacuum fluctuations (Section 3), but amplified and structured.

### 13. Energy

> **Energy is not spent on overcoming distance.**

The object does not cross ordinary space but changes its binding along an existing path. The object's energy (mass, internal and kinetic energy, bond energy, momentum) does not disappear, reset to zero, or convert into transition energy. Transition changes the coordinate, not the energy content.

Three things must be distinguished:

- **Transition energy** (for distance) — practically zero.
- **Synchronization energy** (measurement, computation, AI, modulators, cooling, stability) — essential. The synchronizer is not a perpetual motion machine.
- **The object's own energy** — is conserved.

The cost of transition is determined by the complexity of the object: the number of states, synchronization precision, AI power, modulator efficiency, and preparation time. For homogeneous objects, the number of states grows with mass, so roughly, cost grows with mass but is independent of distance. A neighboring system and a system millions of light-years away cost the same; the main difference is whether an anchor exists.

### 14. Momentum, Velocity, and Causality

**Momentum.** Transition changes *where* the object is, not *how* it moves. Momentum, inertia, and the vector of motion are preserved relative to the anchor. If a ship was traveling at 10 km/s relative to the departure anchor, it will exit at 10 km/s relative to the destination anchor, even if the receiving station is moving at 30 km/s in the opposite direction. The difference is absorbed by conventional engines.

- Exit cannot be made instantly undetectable: time is needed for maneuvering and docking. This is a window for interception.
- Transition does not abolish sublight navigation but replaces it on the longest segment.
- How to formally write the energy balance for anchors moving relative to each other is an open question (Section 34).

**Velocity and causality.** The transition is superluminal relative to ordinary space. But this is not faster-than-light travel through ordinary geometry: the object uses a different metric. Time still flows only forward (Section 2), so superluminal movement is possible without traveling into the past.

### 15. Transition Is Not a Weapon

The idea: jump to an anchor near an enemy station at enormous speed and crash into it. This is nearly impossible.

1. **The anchor provides no picture of the terrain.** Only the point is known; where the station is at the moment of arrival is unknown, and data about the system is years out of date.
2. **The vector is set before transition,** in the departure system, while the direction toward the target in the destination system is unknown.

> **Blind targeting is not an attack but a lottery with a catastrophic loss for the attacker.**

Aiming is possible only with reconnaissance, and reconnaissance is possible in only two ways: a scout ship that can return (expensive, and it leaves a trace, Section 12), or your own people and sensors in the target system transmitting data through the channel (Sections 20–21). Such an operation requires your own anchor in a foreign system and is noticeable. This is not impossibility but price.

### 16. Scaling: Zone, Flow, Limit

The main limitation is not distance but the number of states that must be held in alignment. A small object requires a weak synchronizer; a large ship requires a powerful one. A complex structure is harder than a simple one of the same weight. Synchronizer damage increases risk regardless of distance.

The mass transferable in a single transition is limited by synchronizer power: a low-power unit handles tons; a powerful one, thousands of tons. Scaling is an engineering problem. Whether an absolute limit exists is unknown (Section 34).

**Transition zone** — the area around the anchor where an object can be captured or released; its size depends on equipment power. **Transition flow** — a series of transitions in succession (cargo flow); it requires continuous power supply to the synchronizer.

### 17. The Central Principle

> **The anchor sets the spatiotemporal-state coordinate. Modulators change the state. The AI synchronizes the object's components. The first pulse captures the channel. The path enables transition. The second pulse removes the original synchronization. The anchor fixes the new state. Energy is spent not on overcoming distance but on managing state.**

> **It is not energy that moves the object — coherent state enables the change of spatial binding.**

Distance determines the need for an anchor. Object complexity determines the computational cost of transition. Object state determines the possibility of safe fixation.

## Part II. Consequences

These are logical deductions from the basic principles. The sole exception is Section 29: it describes a future discovery that the principles permit but do not derive.

### 18. Wavelike Expansion

Civilization expands in waves.

**The first wave** — ships travel by conventional means, taking centuries, carrying halves of future anchors (Section 6). Upon arrival, the anchor begins operating, and a channel opens between the new colony and the metropolis. This is how the infrastructure map forms: first the nearest system, then the next.

**Subsequent waves** use channels differently. Ships without anchor cargo travel through existing channels to the network frontier. If industry exists there (Section 5), pairs are entangled at the frontier, and conventional flight begins from there rather than from the metropolis. The anchor cargo itself cannot pass through a channel (Section 6), so each new system beyond the frontier remains a century of conventional travel.

Within connected territory, travel is instantaneous. The network frontier crawls at the speed of ships.

> **An anchor is an investment: installation requires conventional flight, but afterward the route exists permanently.**

### 19. Loss of an Anchor

Losing an anchor is a catastrophe for a colony. The anchor is its only link to the metropolis: without it, no transitions, no communication, no supplies. Recovery requires a new ship by conventional means — centuries.

Causes of loss:

- decoherence due to stabilizer failure or aging (Sections 5, 26);
- physical destruction of the anchor (accident, war, sabotage);
- loss of the partner particle on the other side.

Destroying an anchor does not necessarily destroy the colony. It returns the colony to real cosmic isolation. If the colony is not self-sufficient, this can mean collapse.

### 20. Gray Channels, Physical Mail, and Probes

**Illegal channels.** An unregistered anchor creates a route the authorities do not know about. But delivering its half takes the same centuries of conventional flight (Section 6): "quietly installed" does not happen. Using the channel leaves traces: transit trace (Section 12), fluctuations, interference, instrument errors. Therefore, anchor smuggling is an industry in itself.

**Physical mail.** Entanglement does not transmit information, but the path itself can be used to move physical carriers. Without a channel, this is a ship with records, letters, and data flying a conventional course: slow, but without an anchor. For cut-off colonies, this is the only link.

**Probes.** A channel can send a maximally simple craft: a data carrier and a compact modulator for the second pulse that reads the anchor's reference. Computation and the first pulse are performed by external installations on the sending side, so the probe is cheap. The probe is one-way: it cannot return on its own, as it has no synchronizer. A response is sent by a counter-probe from the other station, so both ends of the channel need stations with synchronizers.

- A probe is small, has few states (Section 16), preparation is fast, and transition is cheap.
- A weak trace (Section 12): mass probe launches are harder to detect than ships.
- Cheap but unreliable: a simple modulator may fail at fixation, and data is lost or corrupted. Hence duplicate sends, mail insurance, and cheap counterfeit probes (Section 27).
- A probe is physical: it can be intercepted on approach from the anchor to the station (Section 7).
- Compact modulators are made from the same rare components as large synchronizers (Section 31), so scarcity hits mail too.

Probes are suitable for large data volumes. A fast, narrow stream is provided by the microchannel (Section 21).

### 21. The Communication Microchannel

Transition mechanics work not only for ships but also for single atoms: the smaller the object, the simpler the synchronization (Section 16). The payload here is not a ship but an atom. This is the same anchor mechanics, not new physics.

**Principle.** The transmitter continuously sends atoms through the anchor's path. The receiver illuminates the arrival zone with a pulse that replicates the anchor's reference: this is not measurement but zone support, so atoms arrive where needed. An atom arrives — a mass spectrometer determines its type — a bit is read.

**Hydrogen and helium.** Hydrogen (mass 1) is one; helium (mass 4) is zero. These are the two simplest and most abundant atoms, distinguishable by mass by a factor of four. Mass spectrometry is a classical measurement that requires no quantum coherence.

**The receiver.** This is a small detector at the anchor point, not a station: large objects must not be near the exit point (Section 7). Data travels to the station by conventional communication.

**What it provides.** A continuous data channel instead of sessions. Any station with an anchor is a communication node: the anchor for transition and for communication is the same. A message travels along a different metric, faster than light relative to ordinary space, but without traveling into the past (Section 2).

**What does not change.** Entanglement still does not transmit information: communication occurs through the movement of atoms, not through measuring an entangled state. The channel works only between two anchors. This is physical mail at the atomic level, not telepathy.

**What the trace reveals.** Each atom leaves a trace (Section 12), and its intensity depends on the number of particles. This means a sensitive sensor at the transmitting anchor can in principle distinguish hydrogen from helium — that is, read the bits. How accurately is an open question (Section 34). Eavesdropping is possible only by being physically present at the anchor, but the fact of transmission is always revealed by the trace.

### 22. The Telegraph Model

Each channel is a point-to-point link between two entangled anchors. You cannot send a message "to everyone," and you cannot create a channel without physical delivery of a particle (until the discovery in Section 29). A message does not pass through ordinary space, so it cannot be intercepted in transit. But the receiver can be seized, like a telegraph station, or the trace can be monitored at the anchor (Section 21).

> **This is not the internet or a telephone. This is a telegraph.**

Each line is a separate, irreplaceable resource: lose an anchor, and the link is severed; restoration requires a new delivery. Therefore, communication lines are strategic assets: they are protected, fought over, and their loss is a catastrophe. The network grows slowly, line by line, like the telegraph network of the 19th century. Bandwidth is limited by the number of atoms per second; messages are encoded and decoded by automation.

### 23. The Hub and Network Topology

Star topology is a natural consequence of how anchors are created. Pairs are entangled in the metropolis, where production and deep-space ships exist, and each colony receives its half. The metropolis is the only point with channels to all colonies. A direct channel between colonies A and B is possible only if a pair was entangled and one half delivered by ship — that is, over centuries and by a decision someone must make (Section 24).

A message from A to B goes: A → metropolis → B. This is two channels, and the metropolis sees everything.

- **Hub dependency.** If the hub goes down, all colonies are cut off from each other. The hub is the most protected point of civilization.
- **Power over information.** Whoever controls the hub can fail to pass a message, delay it, read it, and forward it altered.
- **Physical monopoly.** The metropolis does not pass a law banning direct channels. It simply does not send particles and ships to create them, and the colony generally has neither deep-space ships nor stabilizer production.
- **Motivation for decentralization.** The more colonies, the greater the load on the hub, the delays, and the cost of transit.

### 24. Decentralization and Colonial Independence

Colonies do not need to fight the hub for their own routes: they can create their own anchors and send them to other systems. But they run into the same limitation — delivery is by conventional means, centuries.

- Colony A launches a ship with an entangled particle to colony B.
- Two hundred years later, the ship arrives, and a direct channel between A and B appears, bypassing the hub.
- The metropolis loses control over that route.

> **The hub's monopoly is not political but physical. It rests on the fact that direct anchor delivery between colonies takes centuries.**

Independence is possible but slow. While the anchor is in transit, the hub knows the plans and can interfere — for example, "lose" the launch permit. The monopoly is temporary: the network slowly transforms from a star into a mesh structure. The hub loses its monopoly but remains the largest node.

### 25. Expeditions

An expedition is a ship carrying hundreds or thousands of entangled particles, future anchors. This is not just transport but an infrastructure incubator.

**Cargo and crew.** Transition destroys entanglement (Section 6), so cargo with entangled particles cannot be sent through a channel: it travels by conventional means, for centuries. But the expedition has a **link anchor** — a pair, one half of which stays home and the other is mounted on a mobile buoy near the cargo (not on the ship's hull, Section 7). The ship can leave the cargo with the buoy, jump home, change crew, repair, resupply, jump back, and continue. The vector is preserved during transition; the velocity difference is absorbed by engines (Section 14).

> **Cargo does three hundred years. People do not.**

**Two classes of cargo:**

- **Transit** — can be sent through a channel: people, equipment, fuel, data. Entanglement is irrelevant to them, so its loss does not matter.
- **Anchor** — cannot go through a channel: entangled particles, blanks for communication channels. Conventional flight only.

**Risks to cargo.** While the ship is home, the cargo hangs alone in the void: without crew, protection, or monitoring. A stabilizer fails — a particle decoheres, and no one will know until the ship returns. A meteorite pierces the container — the anchors are lost. A stellar flare destroys part of the entanglements. The more often the ship leaves for maintenance, the longer the cargo is unattended; the less often, the longer the crew goes without rotation and repair.

**Emergency protocol.** If the link anchor is lost, the ship selects one anchor from the cargo; which one is determined by the protocol algorithm. Through this anchor, a distress signal is sent via the microchannel (Section 21). Earth prepares a new link anchor and responds the same way: transition is available again.

> **Every anchor in the cargo is not only a future channel for a colony but also a backup emergency channel home.**

**The cost of the protocol.** Every anchor used as an emergency channel will not reach a colony: out of a thousand, 999 remain, and one system is left without a channel to the hub. Three emergencies in three hundred years — three fewer channels. Which anchor to sacrifice is a strategic decision: for the most promising system, or for the one that will get a channel through a neighbor?

**Two non-renewable resources:** anchors for colonies and the link anchor for return. The loss of either is a catastrophe of different scale, and the emergency protocol partially compensates for the loss of the link anchor at the expense of cargo anchors.

### 26. Aging

**Anchor stabilizers** are not eternal. Over time, isolation degrades, precision drops, and entanglement weakens. This is the main cause of gradual anchor loss under stable conditions. Periodic resynchronization extends service life but not indefinitely: sooner or later, an anchor must be replaced, and replacement requires a new pair (Sections 5, 6).

**Synchronizers** also age: the computing system degrades, modulators wear out, and the number of errors and the probability of incomplete fixation grow. A colony can have a fully functional anchor but gradually lose the ability to pass large objects through it. Therefore, maintenance is a matter of physical safety.

### 27. Counterfeit Synchronizers

Cheap or counterfeit components operate unstably. This creates a new class of accidents: a device capable of performing a transition but incapable of guaranteeing fixation.

> **A counterfeit synchronizer is like a faulty engine, only the consequences of failure are far more serious.**

Cheap counterfeit probes are lost more often (Section 20). For a crew, a counterfeit synchronizer means the risk of scattering (Section 11).

### 28. The Philosophical Consequence

If the object is in an intermediate state between universes during transition, belonging to a universe becomes not only a spatial but also a **state property**. The anchor is not just a coordinate but physical evidence of the object's belonging to a specific universe.

Therefore, "home" is not a place but a set of anchors. If all anchors are lost, home is lost, even if the same stars surround you.

**The religious question.** Does the object retain identity in the traditional sense? Is it the "same" person after changing bindings? Does a person have the right to "embed" into the layer's path? Some traditions will see this as an extension of divine design, others as interference with the nature of reality, and still others as technology that does not affect the spiritual sphere, since the object remains physically continuous.

**The ethical question.** Who is responsible if synchronization is imperfect and some atoms did not return to phase? If a counterfeit synchronizer scattered a crew, is it murder or an accident? Is civilization obligated to send a conventional expedition to a colony cut off by anchor loss if it will be in transit for decades?

> **Technology that makes reality switchable raises the question of what it means to "be here" — and who is responsible for it.**

### 29. Addressable Entanglement — The Apogee

**This is not a consequence of the principles but a second-order assumption: a possible future discovery. Its mechanism remains open (Section 34).**

All stories in the universe are stories before the equation: the age of the telegraph, the age of the hub, the age of long flights and lost expeditions. An age when every anchor is a treasure, every channel is a struggle for independence, and every loss is a tragedy.

And somewhere in a laboratory, perhaps in a small colony at the edge of the network, someone finds a way to addressably narrow the path tree without prior physical delivery. Not new physics but a new mathematics of old physics. As Maxwell did not build an electromagnetic wave but wrote the equations that showed it exists.

**What the equation provides.** If at least one working anchor already exists between two systems, new entangled pairs can be created addressably through the existing channel, without physical delivery. One channel spawns dozens.

- **The hub loses its monopoly**, but slowly: only between systems where addressable entanglement hardware already exists.
- **A second wave of expansion — networked.** Within connected territory, the number of channels grows exponentially because each new one is created through an existing one. The network transforms from a star to a mesh in years, not centuries.

**What the equation does not provide.**

- **Settlement of new systems.** It still requires centuries of conventional flight: there is nothing on the other side yet, and hardware and particles are needed on both ends.
- **Direct information transfer.** Entanglement still carries none; the only means of communication are physical carriers and the stream of atoms through an anchor.
- **A cheap anchor at the frontier.** There, an anchor still costs a century of flight.

> **Inside the network — a dense, instantaneous, multipolar civilization. At the frontier — the same three hundred years of flight, the same lost expeditions, the same insurance pools. And the only way to move from one to the other is to fly hundreds of years by conventional means.**

The equation violates none of the basic principles: the layer, the anchor, and synchronization remain.

## Part III. Overlays

These are what civilization builds on top of consequences: economics, politics, logistics. Each overlay follows from consequences but is not mandatory: a specific civilization may arrange things differently.

### 30. Economics

The economy is defined by two facts: transition costs computation and synchronization energy (Section 13), and an anchor costs centuries of delivery.

**The anchor as capital.** An anchor between two systems is not a device but infrastructure, like a bridge or a pipeline. The owner charges for transition: for the complexity and mass of the object, for energy, for urgency.

**AI as an operational resource.** Control over synchronizer production means control over the civilization's transport system. Synchronizers require processors, quantum coprocessors, cooling, rare materials, repair, and upgrades.

### 31. Supply Chains

Supply is two-tiered. The **interstellar** tier goes through anchors: goods are loaded into a container, pass through the anchor, and are unloaded in the target system. This is fast but costs the transition plus the operator's margin. The **intrasystem** tier is conventional transport. Where there is no anchor, only conventional flight remains.

Not everything is worth shipping: cost grows with mass (Section 13), so massive non-urgent cargo like ore is better produced locally. Data, medicines, and rare components go through transition.

**Supply chain vulnerability.** Synchronizers require raw materials from many systems: computing crystals from some deposits, cryogenic systems from others, rare-earth elements for modulators from yet others, quantum coprocessors from factories dependent on several systems. One break in the chain, and synchronizer production drops.

> **A war or catastrophe in one system that mines a needed mineral can paralyze transit across an entire sector.**

There is no need to destroy anchors or block routes: it is enough to disrupt the supply of one critical component, and within months civilization begins losing the ability to pass large objects. Control over raw material sources matters more than control over routes, and sanctions against component manufacturers are more effective than blockades.

### 32. The Economics of Complexity

> **Civilization does not pay for kilometers. It pays for managing states.**

In an ordinary civilization: distance → fuel → cost. Here: object complexity → computation → power consumption → cost. Therefore:

- A small cargo can be cheap regardless of distance (probes, Section 20);
- A huge ship can be expensive even on a short route;
- AI upgrades increase transport capacity;
- A shortage of computing components becomes a shortage of transport.

Energy remains a commodity: synchronization, production, and life support consume it. Whoever controls energy sources (for example, stellar stations) controls the economy, and anchor operators are its clients.

### 33. The Economics of Communication

The microchannel (Section 21) turns the anchor from transport infrastructure into a communication line and creates a separate market.

- **The hub as a transit center** (Section 23): it charges for routing, like the Suez Canal or a railway junction.
- **Channels as assets.** An anchor generates income: charges per bit, per message, per connection. Channel insurance, reserve pairs, and capacity trading emerge.
- **Communication smuggling.** Illegal anchors create channels bypassing the hub: a black market in transit, underground repeaters.
- **Information as a resource.** Channel bandwidth is limited by the number of atoms per second; a colony receiving more data gains a scientific, economic, and military advantage. The hub controls this flow.
- **Monopoly and competition.** While the hub is the only one, the price of transit is high. When direct channels appear (Sections 24, 29), the price falls and the hub loses its margin.

## Part IV. State of the Concept

### 34. Open Questions

1. **Energy balance for relative anchors.** How is the energy balance formally written for anchors moving relative to each other? The model conserves the object's energy but does not specify the accounting for kinetic energy difference between frames of reference.
2. **Absolute scaling limit.** Is there an absolute upper bound on the mass transferable in a single transition, or is it purely an engineering problem solvable with more powerful synchronizers?
3. **Microchannel eavesdropping precision.** How accurately can a sensitive sensor at the transmitting anchor distinguish hydrogen from helium — that is, read bits from the trace? The model states this is possible in principle but does not specify the precision or the equipment required.
4. **Mechanism of addressable entanglement.** Section 29 describes the effect but not the mechanism. How exactly does one narrow the path tree addressably without physical delivery? This is deliberately left open — it is a future discovery within the world, not a gap in the author's understanding.
5. **Decoherence rate.** How fast does an anchor degrade in practice? The model describes the mechanism (stabilizer aging, isolation degradation) but does not set specific timelines, leaving this to authors.
6. **Cost of synchronization vs. object complexity.** The model states that cost grows with the number of states, not with distance. But what is the exact relationship? Is it linear, polynomial, exponential? This affects the economics of the entire universe.
7. **Microchannel bandwidth.** How many atoms per second can a channel carry? This determines data rates and, consequently, the information economy of the entire civilization.
8. **Detection of transit traces.** How long does a trace remain detectable? Does it decay, and if so, how fast? This determines the window for forensic investigation and intelligence gathering.
9. **Stabilizer production requirements.** What specific technologies and materials are needed to build a stabilizer? The model says colonies without developed industry can install but not build anchors — where exactly is the line?
10. **Network growth rate.** How does the network topology change as the number of colonies grows? When does the hub become a bottleneck not just politically but physically?
11. **Interaction between overlapping channels.** What happens when two anchors operate in close proximity? Does the noise from one affect the other? Can traces from different channels be distinguished?
12. **Maximum number of anchors per system.** Is there a physical limit on the number of anchors that can coexist in one system, or is it purely a matter of space and logistics?
13. **Anchor buoy maneuvering.** A mobile anchor on a buoy moves with its own vector. How does this affect transitions? Can buoys be used tactically — for example, to change the exit vector of an arriving ship?
14. **Long-term degradation of the multiverse layer.** Does active use of paths affect the layer itself? Can civilization degrade the very medium it travels through?

### 35. Position: Beneath Physics

This world is built from the bottom up. One assumption — the multiverse layer with a unified temporal field — and one mechanism — entanglement as a path selector. Everything else follows.

The author does not declare how civilizations should be governed, what their cultures look like, or what stories should be told. The author declares how space and state work. Politics, culture, and specific histories are overlays — they can differ, be rebuilt, or not exist at all.

The canon of this world is not a set of events but a set of rules. An event is canon if it does not contradict the rules. An event that contradicts the rules is not canon, regardless of who wrote it or how good the story is.

### 36. Summary

The document describes a universe where:

- Interstellar travel exists but is constrained by the need for entangled anchors, each requiring conventional delivery.
- Expansion is wavelike: centuries of flight followed by instant connection.
- Communication is a telegraph: point-to-point, atom-by-atom, through the same anchors.
- The hub holds a physical monopoly that erodes slowly as colonies build direct links over centuries.
- Every transition leaves a trace; there are no covert operations.
- The cost of transport depends on object complexity, not distance.
- Addressable entanglement — a future discovery — transforms the network from star to mesh but does not eliminate the frontier.

The world is open to authors. The rules are fixed. The stories are not.

## Part V. Creative Principles and Sandbox

### 37. The Principle: Space Will Not Save You

Space is not a backdrop. It is not an empty stage on which human drama unfolds. In this world, space is a constraint, a resource, and an antagonist.

Every limitation in the physics is a story. A lost anchor is a story. A failed second pulse is a story. A trace that reveals a covert operation is a story. A hub that decides which messages reach a colony is a story. A colony that builds a direct channel over two centuries is a story.

What does not belong in canon:

- Solutions that violate the physics for narrative convenience.
- Technologies that bypass anchor limitations without addressing them.
- "Magic" fixes — sudden discoveries that invalidate established constraints without cost.
- Stories where space is merely a backdrop and the physics could be replaced with any other FTL mechanism.

### 38. Story Prompts

**First iteration — stories before the equation.**

1. **The Last Anchor.** A colony's only anchor decoheres. The colony is self-sufficient but cut off. Three hundred years until a replacement ship can arrive. The story of those three hundred years — or the story of the day the replacement arrives and finds something unexpected.

2. **The Cargo That Outlived the Crew.** An expedition ship carries a thousand anchors. The crew rotates every few years via the link anchor. But the link anchor is lost. The emergency protocol activates. One anchor from the cargo becomes the emergency channel. But which one? And what does the crew do when they realize the protocol has consumed the anchor meant for the most promising system?

3. **The Gray Channel.** Someone has been smuggling an anchor for two hundred years. It arrives. Now there is a channel the hub does not know about. Who controls it? What goes through it? And what happens when the hub detects the trace?

4. **The Counterfeit.** A colony bought a cheap synchronizer from an unreliable supplier. It works — mostly. But during a transition, fixation fails partially. The crew arrives alive but damaged. Who is responsible? The manufacturer? The colony government? The crew who chose the cheap option?

5. **The Sabotaged Anchor.** An anchor is destroyed — not by accident but by sabotage. The colony is cut off. The saboteur is on the colony side. But the colony needs the saboteur: they are the only one who knows how to build a replacement stabilizer from local materials. A story about dependence on the person who hurt you.

6. **The Hub's Choice.** The hub receives a distress signal from a distant colony. It can send help — but only by delaying a shipment of anchors to a new system. Saving one colony means delaying expansion by decades. The hub decides. The story is about the decision and its aftermath — for both colonies.

7. **The Probe That Came Back.** A probe was sent decades ago to a system with no anchor. It was not supposed to return — probes are one-way. But it did. It carries data from a system no one has heard from in fifty years. What happened there? And why did the probe come back when it should not have?

8. **The Quiet War.** Two colonies share a hub. The hub controls both channels. One colony wants independence and begins building a direct anchor to the other — a two-hundred-year project. The hub knows. The hub can interfere. But open interference means admitting the colony wants to leave. A story about a cold war fought over centuries, where the weapon is patience.

9. **The Forgotten Expedition.** An expedition was launched three hundred years ago. It carried a thousand anchors. It was supposed to found ten colonies. No one has heard from it since. A trace appears at an anchor point where no trace should be. Someone — or something — is transitioning. But the expedition was supposed to be too far away to use any existing anchor.

10. **The Stolen Anchor.** A colony's anchor is not lost — it is stolen. A ship physically took the anchor half from the colony's station. The colony is cut off. The thief has an anchor that works — but only if the other half still exists on the metropolis side. A story about the one piece of infrastructure that cannot be replaced and the person who stole it.

11. **The Missionary.** A religious figure travels between colonies and teaches that transition is a divine act — the soul passes through the layer and returns. Then a transition fails. The missionary is scattered — partially. The body is recovered but is not entirely "here." A story about faith tested by the physics it tried to sanctify.

12. **The Last Message.** A colony is losing its anchor. Decoherence is irreversible. The colony has months of microchannel left. They send one last message through the atom stream. It is a short message. The story is about what they chose to say — and what the metropolis heard.

13. **The Equation.** The apogee. Someone, somewhere, finds the mathematics that enables addressable entanglement. The world changes — not overnight, but inexorably. The hub falls, the network meshes, and the frontier remains. This is the story that ends the first iteration and opens the second. Everything before it was the age of the telegraph. Everything after it is the age of the network.

### 39. Canon Consistency Model

Canon in this universe is not a hierarchy of authority but a consistency check. There are two levels:

**Hard canon — the physics.** Parts I and II of this document. Not subject to vote, committee decision, or author popularity. The physics either works or it does not. A story that violates the physics is not canon, period.

**Soft canon — events.** What happened, in what order, and to whom. Established by published works and maintained by the arbiter. Contradictions between works are resolved by the arbiter, not by retcon.

**Stage 1 — the concept author as arbiter.** Until the community grows, the concept author serves as sole arbiter of both hard and soft canon. The author reviews works for physics compliance and resolves event contradictions. The author cannot change the physics to accommodate a story.

**Stage 2 — a collegium of authors.** As the community develops, a collegium forms to manage soft canon. The collegium votes on event conflicts, continuity, and new canonical events. The concept author retains veto power over changes to hard canon — that is, over the physics. The collegium does not vote on physics.

**Canon procedure.**

1. A work is published or submitted.
2. The author or collegium checks it against hard canon (physics compliance).
3. If it passes, it is checked against soft canon (event consistency).
4. If it passes both, it is canon. If it fails either, the author is informed of the specific contradiction.
5. The author may revise and resubmit. Canon status is never automatic.

### 40. The AI Assistant as Editor

An AI assistant trained on this document can serve as a first-pass editor for authors writing in this universe. The assistant does not write stories, make creative decisions, or determine canon status. It performs three checks:

**Before writing.** The author describes the premise. The assistant checks: does this premise require physics that the model does not support? Does it assume capabilities the anchors or synchronizers do not provide?

**During writing.** The author can ask the assistant to verify specific scenes: does this transition work? Would this trace be detectable? Can this character survive this failure mode? The assistant answers with reference to the document and flags contradictions.

**After writing.** The assistant performs a full consistency check: physics compliance, timeline consistency, terminology accuracy. It produces a report of potential contradictions. The author decides what to fix. The assistant does not override the author's creative choices — it only marks where the physics or canon breaks.
